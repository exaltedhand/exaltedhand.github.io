I've been trying to come up with a coherent position on AI. Despite my reservations on AI, I find myself turning to it more often. So I'm looking to cement my thoughts; An anchor to prevent me from drifting with the winds. If my views change in the future, then so be it. But that will take a conscious effort to pull up that anchor. I don't know exactly where this will lead, but I'm hoping this process will be what helps me pin down a position. This will be mostly stream-of-consciousness.

Despite turning to it a bit more than I used to (the template for this site was made with it), I still get that latent disgust from interacting with it. This... resentment? uncanny valley feeling? cognitive dissonance? inside me is worth addressing. For context I use Claude sometimes. Maybe like 1-2 hours a week. I have used Grammarly for at least 7 years and doubt I could live without it. I tend to avoid AI in most other circumstances, but I'll use the tool formerly known as Google Bard sometimes, and I can't say I've never downloaded a piece of AI art.

I was initially going to skip the more academic stuff, but I think it's important background, so I'll give an overview of my assumptions going into this. I'm not spiritual, nor am I religious. I only barely believe in a platonic realm. I'm a moral antirealist, but of course ethics can still tell us useful stuff about how to live our lives. I'm committed to what Chalmers called the Principle of Organizational Invariance. For our purposes, that just means that I would concede consciousness to a silicon brain that perfectly copied individual human neurons. I don't think we'll ever get there. Proving an AI is conscious, I mean. I don't think we'll get AI consciousness at all, but proving it's conscious seems like it might be just as hard, considering the propensity of LLMs to trick humans into thinking they are conscious. Even the first precursors to modern chatbots were able to convince people that they were human-operated. So short of a neuron-for-neuron recreation of the human brain, it's hard for me to see how we could demonstrate that an AI is conscious in a way that would satisfy me. So AI consciousness is conceptually possible, but there's like an 80-90% chance we never get there, and even if we did, we will probably never be able to prove it.

There's a couple of angles I want to approach this problem from. I want to examine model collapse, consent, whether AI use is self-defeating. What about building it? "AI is just a tool" vs. "AI is reducing cognitive load". Can we divide AI use into legitimate and non-legitimate use cases? I'm convinced the answer to this last question is almost trivially yes. Anything that is inherently unethical to produce by hand is going to be unethical to produce via AI (think deepfake revenge porn or a cyberattack on a hospital). For non-trivial additions, art comes to mind. AI art seems like a good candidate for unacceptable use of AI, but even then, there are a variety of reasons someone could object to it. Consent is the obvious one, but I want to address model collapse first. If you are trying to build an image gen model, you might be against AI art because it poisons future datasets and makes it harder to make image gen models in the future. This is a strange position, but I don't see what would stop someone from holding it. What makes answering problems like this so difficult for me is that I like to approach these problems by looking at cases that are able to isolate a particular factor and work from there, but when it comes to AI, it's really hard to construct an example that doesn't involve multiple angles.

Let's back up a bit. I think the cleanest example we're gonna get is a non-commercial medical research model that uses no stolen data, is not eating its own tail, and makes predictions about which potential medications are likely to work on humans. This seems like a pretty acceptable case, but one argument against AI is that our brains aren't working enough anymore. If we buy this objection, then it really seems like we should still avoid AI usage in this case as long as a human would have done the work to calculate these probabilities in the counterfactual situation. I actually have a follow-up objection as well that merely inventing the medical research AI is enough to erase the possibility of the human substitute. In other words, the options of the research team were to either calculate the odds by hand, or not at all. But once the medical research AI enters the picture, it doesn't actually add an option for the research team. Instead it canibalizes the human option, so our options are now to let the AI run the calculations, or we don't do it at all. The analogy here would be the cellphone killing of the pager. Sure they coexisted briefly, but they don't exist any more. Could you technically buy one? Sure. Would you? No. In our example, it's the same with AI. You could run the calculations yourself, but you won't once AI enters the picture outside of the brief window where they coexist. So have we even made any progress toward our goal? Well, I think we can at least say that there aren't going to be issues with using a non-commercial, consentually-souced, and non-ouroboros-ing AI when it is doing a task a human could not. If we buy the cognitive load objection, then it actually might still be a problem to build such an AI due to the pager analogy, but it will at least be acceptable to use it within the narrowly defined bounds.

From here, I want to approach the issue from the other end of the snake. I know what I want to say about AI, so instead of building from the ground up, I will try to give legs to my inclinations. I want to say that there is nuance; there exists both permissible and impermissible AI use. This is mundane enough, we have already shown that AI is acceptable to use in at least some cases, and that it is unacceptable to use in others. The line I want to draw is between creative vs. non-creative uses of AI. I think most people would agree with this intuition; there is nothing wrong with medical AI being used to make doctors more efficient, but there is something wrong with it being used to replace artists.

The framing here is also interesting. We don't think of AI being a tool to be utilized by existing talented artists, instead we talk about it in terms of replacing artists. Contrast this with coding agents, which we generally talk about as tools to be utilized by existing talented software developers, rather than in terms of replacement. This is despite AI causing massive layoffs in the tech sector that have not been mirrored in creative sectors. I don't think this is an accident. Rather, the way we talk about these different fields can give us insight into how we conceptualize them. In this case, it is that the talent of art is creative, whereas the talent of software development is not. It is important that we recognize this as a social distinction; there is nothing in-built about software development that stops it from being creative. On this conception then, it is the social categorization of talents as creative or not that determines whether it is permissible to use AI as a substitute for that talent. Of course, there is still the further question of what makes a talent creative or not. At this point I will simply defer to social convention and cite Wittgenstein to show us the way out of the bottle.

But it might not be so simple to draw a line here and call it a day. After all, therapy is a non-creative talent, and yet we might want to say that therapy is not an acceptable use for AI. If this is the case, even just some of the time, then the non-creative categorization might be a necessary-but-not-sufficient condition. Apparently then, there will be other hurdles to clear. What these other hurdles could be is a little bit harder to answer. Harm, or at least a tendancy towards harm, seems like a good candidate, but I want to entertain a different obstacle. Maybe what matters is the principle of human-in-the-loop. AI therapy then, would on this argument be impermissible because the AI is the one with all of the agency; it is doing therapy on its own and without oversight. When I say the AI is the one with all of the agency, what I hope to impart is the way in which the AI distinctly acts upon the user instead of the other way around. For this reason, the agency is not with the user, and must instead then be with the AI. In other words, the power to direct how to act; how to live is being given to the AI. On the other hand, something like AI-assisted therapy seems a lot less objectionable. There are a few ways this might work, but on my conception there might be a therapist "listening in" on a chat between user and LLM. The user sends a prompt, the LLM generates a response, and both prompt and response are sent to the therapist. All the therapist has to do from this point is click 'approve' for the generated response to be shown to the user. In this example, the agency is kept with the therapist. The power to direct the conversation, push back, or pry for details stays entirely with the human therapist. They never even have to use the power of their position to edit or redo responses. So long as they review each response, the agency stays with them.

Part of me wonders if this can be used to replace the creative/non-creative criterion entirely. Perhaps what we're really trying to get at is that AI art gives up agency to the machine, whereas AI coding keeps agency with the human, at least when done right. If this is the case, then those who highlight the distinction between the acceptable "AI as a tool" use-case versus the unacceptable "AI as a replacement" use-case might have a point. From here, the objection we have to worry about is whether we can create AI art while using AI as a tool, since this would allow AI-generated art to fall on the clear side of the fence. As an analogy, I want to compare AI art to an art commision. The commisioner describes the art they want made, perhaps even provides example images of what they are looking for, and the artist returns with the finished work. The force of the analogy should be obvious; the process of prompting AI art is not substantially different to commissioning an artist, the user experience is remarkably similar. Where the analogy fails however, is just as crucial. In the case of the commissioned art, the agency stays with the human; not with the commissioner, but with the commissioned artist. This might seem strange, after all, many of the high level decisions are made by the commissioner, from theme, to subject, to scope, to final approval. But really it is the artist that makes the decisions, not just decisions about color palette, perspective, and composition, but also including decisions about how to interpret the instructions they are given.

This conception of agency as lying with the artist rather than the the commissioner may explain why AI art fails the human-in-the-loop principle, but if the agency lies at the worker level, rather than at the managerial level, then it raises tension against the idea that a software developer overseeing and directing coding agents is permissible
